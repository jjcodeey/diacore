Renal Grocery List

This list combines foods that may fit a renal diet. Eat a balanced variety in appropriate portions. Individual needs differ, especially with diabetes, heart disease, weight concerns, laboratory results, and dialysis treatment. Follow the plan from your renal dietitian or doctor.

If you have diabetes, limit carbohydrate portions of starches, fruit, and sweets as directed. These food groups are marked with an asterisk (*).

Starches

Whole-grain versions may be appropriate for some people. Ask your dietitian whether they fit your plan.

  • Plain bagels; white, French, sourdough, or rye bread; plain breadsticks
  • Ready-to-eat cereals such as Rice Krispies, Puffed Rice, Rice Chex, and Cornflakes
  • Cooked cereals such as Cream of Wheat or Rice, Farina, and Malt-o-Meal
  • Couscous, unsalted crackers, graham or animal crackers, croissants, dinner rolls, English muffins, and grits
  • Hamburger or hotdog buns, Melba toast, pita bread, pasta and noodles, unsalted popcorn and pretzels
  • White rice, rice cakes, and flour tortillas

Vegetables

  • Alfalfa or bean sprouts, asparagus, canned bamboo shoots, canned beets, bell peppers, cabbage, cauliflower, celery, cucumber, and dandelion greens
  • Eggplant, endive or escarole, green beans, hominy, jalapenos, jicama, leeks, lettuce, raw mushrooms, and okra
  • Onions, radishes, seaweed or kelp, spaghetti squash, summer squash (such as crookneck), tomatillos, canned water chestnuts, and wax beans

Fruits

  • Apples, applesauce, canned apricots, blackberries, blueberries, boysenberries, cherries, cranberries, and cranberry sauce
  • Canned fruit cocktail, grapes, kumquat, lemon, lime, loganberries, lychees, canned mandarin oranges, plums, raspberries, rhubarb, strawberries, tangerines, and watermelon
  • Canned pears, canned peaches, and pineapple

Meat / Protein

  • Beef, chicken, egg whites, fish, lamb, fresh pork, soft tofu, low-sodium tuna, turkey, shellfish, and wild game

Beverages

  • Apple juice, cranberry juice, cream soda, fruit punch, ginger ale, grape juice or soda, and horchata
  • Kool-Aid or Hi-C, lemonade, lemon-lime soda (such as Sprite or 7-Up), limeade, mineral water, Mountain Dew, and nectars (apricot, peach, pear)
  • Orange soda, pineapple juice, root beer, Sunny Delight citrus punch*, and tea

Fats

  • Butter or margarine, cream cheese, mayonnaise, sour cream, and vegetable oils such as canola or olive oil

Sweets

  • Apple butter; cake (sponge, angel, pound, spice, yellow, or lemon); candy corn; chewing gum; cinnamon drops; cookies (sugar, shortbread, gingersnap, or lemon cream)
  • Cotton candy; doughnuts without nuts or chocolate; fruit ice; gelatin; gumdrops; hard candy; honey; jam or jelly; jellybeans; maple syrup
  • Marmalade, marshmallows, peppermints, apple/cherry/lemon pie, fruit-flavored popsicles, Rice Krispie treats, red licorice, sorbet, sugar, vanilla wafers, and vanilla cupcakes

Seasonings

  • Allspice, basil, bay leaf, black pepper, caraway seed, cardamom, chili powder, chives, cilantro, cinnamon, cloves, coriander, crushed red pepper flakes, cumin, and curry powder
  • Dill, vanilla/almond/peppermint extracts, fennel, garlic (fresh or powder), ginger, horseradish, lemon or lime juice, liquid smoke, mint, mustard, nutmeg, and onion (fresh, powder, or flakes)

Sodium Management

Sodium is found in table salt and many everyday foods. Too much may increase thirst and blood pressure and cause the body to hold extra fluid. For dialysis patients, extra fluid can build up between sessions.

Choose foods with less salt

  • Limit instant noodles, salty snacks, processed meats (such as hotdogs, ham, and bacon), canned or preserved foods, fast food, and ready-made meals.
  • Limit salty sauces and seasonings, including soy sauce, fish sauce, and seasoning cubes.

Ways to reduce salt intake

  • Use less salt when cooking and limit soy sauce, fish sauce, and seasoning cubes.
  • Choose fresh foods over highly processed foods; taste food before adding more salt.
  • Use garlic, onion, ginger, lemon, or herbs for flavor.

Lower-sodium alternatives

Instead ofConsider
Processed meats such as hotdogs and hamFresh chicken or fish prepared with less salt

Read sodium food labels

  • Find “Sodium” on the Nutrition Facts label and check the serving size.
  • Compare similar products and choose the option with less sodium per serving when appropriate.
  • Count servings. For example, two servings containing 200 mg each provide 400 mg sodium.

Eating less salt may reduce thirst. Follow your prescribed fluid allowance, use measured sips, and ask your healthcare team before using salt substitutes; some contain potassium.

Potassium & Chronic Kidney Disease

Potassium supports nerve and muscle function, including the heart. Healthy kidneys balance potassium. When kidney function is reduced, potassium may build up in the blood. High potassium can cause weakness, numbness, tingling, or dangerous heart rhythm problems. Your doctor monitors levels and your renal dietitian individualizes food and portion guidance. Dialysis removes some potassium, but food choices still matter between treatments.

Reference note: The source guide cites 2,000–2,500 mg per day as a general range for some people with kidney disease. This is not a personal target; follow the amount set by your clinician.

Phosphorus Management

Phosphorus helps keep bones and teeth strong. When kidneys cannot remove enough, it can build up in the blood and affect bones and other tissues. Food choices depend on laboratory results and the individual care plan.

Choose foods with less phosphorus

  • Ask whether to limit processed meats, cola drinks and some processed beverages, large servings of dairy, nuts, seeds, certain beans, and packaged foods with phosphate additives.
  • You do not need to avoid every food on a list; match portions and choices to your dietitian’s plan.

Food alternatives

Instead ofConsider
Processed meats with phosphate additivesFresh chicken or fish prepared safely
Cola with phosphorus additivesWater or another kidney-friendly drink within the fluid allowance
Large dairy servingsDietitian-approved alternatives in appropriate portions
Packaged foods with phosphate additivesFresh, less-processed foods when available

Read labels and follow the care plan

  • Look for ingredient words containing “phos,” such as phosphoric acid or sodium phosphate.
  • Compare similar products and choose those with fewer phosphate additives when possible.
  • Keeping phosphorus in range may help protect bones and teeth and support long-term kidney care.
  • Take prescribed phosphate binders exactly as directed, usually with meals or snacks if instructed. Do not start, stop, or change medications without medical advice.
  • Attend dialysis and laboratory monitoring appointments; ask your renal dietitian when unsure about a food label.

Fluid Management Guide for Dialysis Patients

Fluid management means tracking liquids each day. When kidneys cannot remove extra fluid effectively, it may build up and cause swelling, high blood pressure, or difficulty breathing. Follow the daily fluid allowance recommended by your doctor or renal dietitian.

Managing daily fluid intake

  • Measure drinks, use a small cup, and spread drinks throughout the day.
  • Soup, ice, ice cream, and gelatin may count toward the daily allowance depending on the care plan.
  • Do not change a prescribed fluid limit without consulting your healthcare team.

Tips for managing thirst

  • Reduce salty foods; rinse your mouth with water and spit it out instead of swallowing.
  • Use small ice chips only if they fit within the allowance.
  • Sugar-free gum or hard candy may help if appropriate for you; take small, measured sips.

Recognize signs of fluid overload

  • Swelling of feet, ankles, hands, or face; sudden weight gain between sessions; increased blood pressure; shortness of breath; or unusual discomfort/bloating.

Urgent: Seek medical attention for severe or sudden difficulty breathing or chest pain. Ask your care team how much fluid to drink, which foods count, and report difficulty managing thirst.
